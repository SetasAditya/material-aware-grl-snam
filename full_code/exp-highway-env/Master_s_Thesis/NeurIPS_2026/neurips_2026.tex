\begin{abstract}
Risk-aware navigation should be selective: a policy should expose evasive degrees of freedom only when the local scene admits a lower-risk feasible maneuver, and suppress them when no safer alternative exists. We show that adding one context-energy term to a port-Hamiltonian navigation policy produces a learned force channel with exactly this falsifiable signature. When the local risk field contains a feasible lower-risk direction, the induced context force activates toward it; when the apparent escape is blocked or not yet available, a route-aware gate suppresses lateral force rather than hallucinating an unsafe maneuver. A CVaR tail-risk objective focuses gradient updates on rare but consequential risk transitions. We validate the selectivity signature across four settings. In the primary delayed-required-escape benchmark, route-aware CVaR reduces premature force activation from 0.950 to 0.180 versus DWA while raising success from 0.480 to 0.810 with zero replans. On real off-road terrain (RELLIS-3D), route-aware enrichment achieves correct activation rate 0.837 and false activation rate 0.114, compared to 0.378/0.752 for scalar risk gradients. On static semantic maps (DFC2018), enrichment reduces catastrophic failure from 0.60 to 0.10 and oscillation by 90.7\% while preserving path efficiency. In highway traffic, collisions drop from 100\% to 0\% when a lane escape is feasible; when no escape exists, the policy suppresses the lateral maneuver. The selectivity property follows from the gradient structure of the context energy rather than from training-time tuning.
% Risk-aware navigation requires agents to adapt before evolving hazards make safe control infeasible. However, many existing methods encode risk as a static planning cost or apply it reactively only after unsafe behavior becomes imminent, making them brittle when risk changes during execution. We introduce a continuous policy framework that learns a material-aware Hamiltonian risk field whose gradients define navigation updates. The learned field couples goal progress, barrier constraints, and material-dependent exposure in a unified energy-based formulation, allowing newly observed risk information to reshape the local control landscape without switching among discrete actions or replanning from scratch. To improve robustness to rare but consequential transitions, we optimize a CVaR objective and train with a curriculum of failure-inducing scenarios, encouraging the policy to preserve safety margins against plausible future risks rather than only currently observed ones. We evaluate performance using time-integrated violation severity, which measures not only whether safety constraints are violated but also how severely and for how long. On DFC2018-derived material-aware traversal, our method reduces cumulative risk exposure by 56.4\% relative to a geometry-only baseline and by 17.4\% relative to material-blind Dijkstra, while producing less oscillatory trajectories. In highway-env slow-leader escape scenarios, collisions drop from 100\% to 0\% when a feasible lane escape exists; when no safe escape is available, the policy correctly avoids spurious evasive maneuvers. Across both domains, continuous Hamiltonian risk-field updates produce fewer, shorter, and less severe violations than discrete and reactive baselines, demonstrating that the method improves safety throughout the trajectory rather than merely reducing average path risk.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

One additive context-energy term gives a port-Hamiltonian policy a falsifiable
selectivity signature: activate when a safer feasible alternative exists and
suppress otherwise.  Ablations isolate Hamiltonian structure, CVaR tail timing,
and context-conditioned coefficients; the pattern holds across off-road,
semantic, dynamic, and highway settings without global replanning.


\paragraph{Social Impact Statement}
This work uses public/simulated navigation data and no private information. It
aims to reduce navigation risk, but is not a safety certificate; deployment
requires independent validation, fallback control, and monitoring.
